Read the Towers blurb...


In the spring of 1994, something terrible has awakened in Dawesville, Australia.

Fish are dying in the estuary, and strange disappearances are plaguing the usually tranquil small town.

When the younger sister of local 'it' girl Mary Macy vanishes, amateur detective Laura Harris and her reluctant twin brother, Johnno, finally have the case they've been waiting for.

As the town's disappearances mount, their unlikely group only grows. Mary joins them, desperate to find her sister. So does Kayden Ty, searching for his missing father.

But some things, once taken by the estuary, don't easily come back. And what's out there in the depths didn't wake up on its own.

Stephen King's small-town dread meets Twin Peaks' surreal menace — with a twist of dry Australian wit. Towers is the first instalment in a mystery series that will make you afraid of the water, and those that lurk around it... forever.

Chapter 1

The creature—for that’s all he could think to call it—was humming a tune. At least that’s what it sounded like through the vibration of the cool night air. That was the last thing he remembered as water filled his ears and his shoulder hit the warm muck and darkness that lay on the bottom of the estuary bed.

Looking upwards from where he lay in the silt, he could just make out the red signal light on top of the Eastern Water Tower, gently flashing above him. The creature was finished—it was moving away from him now. Although it hadn’t been painful. Almost peaceful once he had accepted no miracle was coming to save him. As the life slipped from his eyes, he watched the huge torso of the creature flex itself toward the mouth of the concrete overflow pipe that connected to the tower above him. With a final flick of its webbed tail, it was gone.


Chapter 2

‘Jesus, Laura, will you shut up?’ Johnno stopped dead and swung round to face her.

She skidded to a halt to avoid running into him. ‘What is your problem tonight?’ The tape recorder in her hand whirred to a stop.

‘Believe it or not, you’re not actually FBI Special Agent Dale Cooper. This isn’t some TV show; we’re not in Twin Peaks. This is Dawesville. If Ty Senior catches us following him, we’re going to be in serious trouble, all right?’

Ty Senior had a nasty reputation, sure; Johnno had a point. Although seeing his red hair plastered against his pale forehead, the whites of his eyes popping in the moonlight, she decided she didn’t care. She grinned, then extended her free hand to him.

‘What?’ Johnno stared.

‘You can hold my hand if you like.’

He kicked the ground, looking down. ‘I’m not scared, it’s just that—’

She didn’t wait for him to finish, hiding her smirk as she pushed a low-hanging branch out of the way and continued towards the darkly glistening water, raising the tape recorder back to her mouth as she went, ‘Detective Laura Harris resuming her report. It’s nearly midnight on the first of September 1994. Conditions on the southern edge of the Dawesville Estuary are clear, a full moon providing good visibility. We are currently pursuing Daniel Ty, the suspect —’

She heard Johnno pick his way toward where she was crouched, hidden behind a small bank of reeds on the water’s edge. He hissed in her ear as he placed his bag carefully onto the sand, ‘Will you at least whisper?’

She lowered her voice. ‘Who is approximately thirty meters offshore and appears to be fishing. Although the navigation lights on his boat are switched off, which is, naturally, suspicious.’

Johnno snorted, ‘There’s no way he’s out here just fishing. I’m ninety-nine per cent sure that man over there murdered Andrew Stanton. Ty Senior was the last person seen with him. I mean seriously, what more do you need?’

Laura frowned at him. Yes, Andrew Stanton had gone missing. Yes, Ty Senior was the prime suspect, but the police report had specifically noted a lack of hard evidence. ‘Well, we’ll still need something to connect him to it. And ideally it’ll come in the form of a body.’

Johnno clicked his tongue and reached into his bag, pulling out a pair of heavy binoculars for her. ‘Well, come on then.’ He took out his much-prized birdwatching camera and drew the camouflage hood tight around his head. ‘Let’s catch ourselves a god damn killer.’

Murderer or not, it was clear that Ty Senior worked with a practised hand; the whistle of his line arced across the estuary again and again, catching nothing more than dripping weed each time. This must have continued for at least an hour, because, before Laura could check her watch, Johnno nudged her and she realised her legs had gone numb and she had slipped half-asleep.

‘Get ready, looks like he’s about to move.’

Laura gently rocked side to side, coming out of her reverie—binoculars pressing into her cheeks as she squinted at the boat—cursing herself for drifting off. She watched him reach for the motor, his cigarette balanced in his mouth as he worked the starter cord, lifting his cap to wipe the sweat from his brow as the engine finally spluttered to life.

‘What’s he doing?’ Johnno breathed. ‘Has he seen us?’

‘Shut up.’ She hissed back at him. Ty Senior was easing the boat directly toward where they were hidden. He cut the engine and let the boat glide for a moment, then, in one swift movement, grabbed the gaff from its holder on the gunwale and leaned over the bow to catch a line of rope floating just below the surface. He was maybe ten meters away now, close enough that Laura didn’t need the binoculars to make out what was happening. A largeish mesh cage, the size of a carry-on suitcase, thunked against the side of the boat—she could see now it was part of a series of ten or so buoys—as he hauled it aboard. Water poured from it, pooling on the deck. ‘Crab pots. He was banned from crabbing last year but.’

‘I know, it was in the Herald.’ Johnno whispered back, clicking off several photos. They watched him, his back to them, lift the mesh flap of the first pot and push something inside, wrapped tightly in black plastic.

Laura glanced at Johnno, almost sure they were having the same thought. Is that… chopped-up bits of Andrew Stanton? She nudged him. ‘We need to get a look in one of those pots’. He shot back a look to ask if she was crazy. ‘Think about it, the estuary isn’t actually that deep here. We can come back as soon as it’s light with a mask and dive weights.’ He opened his mouth to reply, but before he could, a terrible scream twisted out across the water. It came again, louder this time, and directly behind them. Laura spun around, careful not to give away their position, scanning the dark bush behind her, every shape and shadow now a threat.

A few moments passed. Johnno, panting, struggled to get the words out. ‘I don’t see. Anyone.’

As if in response, a pale shape burst from the canopy and swept directly toward them. They flattened themselves into the ground, as Johnno let out a laugh of relief. ‘It’s an owl. I can’t believe I didn’t recognise the call.’

‘Careful, quiet.’ Laura hissed.

‘Sorry. They’re really rare, you know,’ he whispered, following the gliding shape with his camera, watching it as it reached the opposite bank of the estuary and landed on a branch with a soft flurry of its wings. ‘Beautiful.’

Laura looked back at the boat—her heart slowly thumping back to normal— then cursed. At what point it had happened, she wasn’t sure, and whether it had been done deliberately or not, she didn’t know; but Daniel Ty was gone. She stood up, throwing all pretence of stealth out the window. ‘Where did he go? We looked away for maybe thirty seconds!’

‘Do you think he swam to shore?’ Johnno asked, eyeing the water nervously. ‘Maybe we should get out of here.’

‘No, wait here. If he comes for us, just start swinging or something.’ She swept the binoculars across the water again, muttering to herself. ‘Come on… come on… where are you?’ The boat was still there, the gaff leaning across the gunwale, a thin curl of smoke rising from the cigarette balanced in the tab of an open beer can. Laura lowered the binoculars. ‘If you were going to slip away from someone. Do you think you’d finish your cigarette first?’

Johnno looked over. ‘Probably. Out of habit, I guess.’

‘Look at this.’

Johnno scanned the scene, taking a beat to gather his thoughts. ‘People don’t just vanish into thin air. I mean, he must have gone somewhere.’

A moment later, a splash exploded to their right. Laura only just caught it out of the corner of her eye: a giant column of water, at least three meters tall. It hung in the air for a moment, then crashed down. ‘Go!’ She shouted.

Johnno was already ahead of her, sprinting through the scrub that lined the water’s edge towards the place where the column had been, the camera bouncing wildly from the strap around his neck. They skidded to a halt. Johnno, with his hands on his knees, panted, ‘What was that?’

Blinking the last white dots of exertion from her eyes, she carefully approached the edge of the water. It was slack now, glassy enough for her to peer into the shallows. She caught herself at the last second, reeling back as a hundred eyes stared back at her. ‘Fish,’ she called. ‘Dead fish. Hundreds of them.’ They looked silver in the moonlight, no more than ten centimetres long and five wide. Nothing natural could have done that; the scene was as odd as it was terrible. Thinking back, she wasn’t sure if it was the terror or the strangeness that made her do what she did next: grimacing as the icy water flooded her boots.

‘Careful!’ Johnno cautioned. ‘Laura. Please. Don’t go any deeper.’

‘Don’t worry, they can’t die twice.’ She called, reaching into the water and gingerly picking one up. It felt hard in her hand. Like it had been lying in the sun for many days. She tossed it toward Johnno. ‘Get a photo of that.’

His hand shook slightly as he placed it in the shot as a reference for size. ‘Do you think whatever did this also…’ he trailed off.

Laura searched around the area where the fish lay, the water turning a milky white. Her brow was furrowed as she looked for any evidence or reason to explain what they had just seen. A flash of red caught her eye. She toed the sand where she had seen it. It was a cap—bright red. Johnno took another photograph, his jaw clenching and unclenching. He recognised it immediately.

Laura raised the tape recorder to her lips, her finger over the record button. After a moment, she lowered it again and looked directly at Johnno. ‘I think we need to call Dad.’


Chapter 3

Dick Harris didn’t particularly like working the night shift at the station. It wasn’t the lack of sleep that bothered him; as a Sergeant, he had long since learnt the trick to staying alert—although his children always complained the next day that it made his breath smell like rotting cardboard. It wasn’t even the odd calls he had to take that bothered him—a drunken couple fighting, reports of lost dogs (that he would usually find sometime later, dead on the highway), or some depressed bastard who had drunk too much of something and wandered into the bush. He sighed, tilting his hat further over his eyes. No, it was the goddamn lighting that bothered him most. The new fluorescent tubes they had installed in the tiny office they called Dawesville Station made him feel sick. He hoped they would soon centralise operations back to the City of Mandurah. He spread his fingers wide and placed his palms on either side of the report he was trying to edit, willing himself back to focus.

The car was stolen at approximately nine-thirty on Tuesday morning. The suspect, male, mid-thirties, in a dark tracksuit jumper—he stared at his hands; his skin looked half green, like it had been dipped in dog piss. He shook his head, glancing around for a distraction. No wonder everyone hated cops; we’re all pissed off all the time. He groaned and eased himself out of his chair. Midupward stretch, the radio alarm warbled at him from across the station. He glanced down at his wrist—the numbers on his watch glowed one-thirty. Who on earth could be calling at this time?He walked toward the receiver and snatched it from its cradle.

‘Harris.’ He barked. There was nothing but static on the other end. Then, a voice, breathless and very small, crackled through.

‘Dad?’

‘Laura? What’s wrong?’ His voice turned to worry almost immediately. She didn’t say anything for at least five full seconds before he broke the silence. ‘Are you in trouble?’

‘No. Not exactly.’

‘Elaborate.’ His initial concern evaporating.

‘We’re at the estuary, on the southern side. We were investigating—’

He didn’t wait to hear the rest. He barked a final order at her, ‘Stay there; I’m on my way.’ Then signed off. Grabbing his jacket from the hanger as he strode toward the 75’ Troop Carrier parked outside the station, the door banging close behind him.

The cool night air did little to calm him down. Muttering to himself as he pressed the accelerator, the car roaring under him. ‘Can’t believe… explicitly told them not to… investigating again.’ He swung off the main street and cut toward the estuary, picking up speed as he left town. He cursed David Lynch and Kyle McGlocklan, or whatever the fuck his name was. Ever since they had watched that stupid show last summer, it had been investigations this, disappearances that. I mean, seriously. What teenage kid spends their time casually researching murders? No. This was the limit. It was time to put his foot down. He swore. Damn it.Whatever they had done. There would be no more after this. His brow now set, a cloud over his forehead. He counted the trunks of the trees rushing past, trying to calm himself down. Onetwothreefourfivesix… eightnineteneleven —his mind thrust back to his childhood, his mother turning round to face him in the back seat.

‘Don’t worry darling, we’ll be home in no time. Just count the Wellingtonias, there’s a good boy.’ His father’s Surry laugh carrying him to sleep before he realised they were back at the house every time.

What had he done wrong? He had been afraid of his parents, unable to disappoint them, yes, of course. That was how it was supposed to be. This country was certainly no England. Or maybe children these days were just different. They think they’re adults, like they have rights, opinions. Entitlements. Well, he thought, if that’s what they want—reaching down to flick on sirens on— that’s what they’ll get.


Chapter 4

Laura heard the sirens long before she saw them. Grimacing at Johnno as he looked up from where he was sitting on an upturned log, his head in his hands. They knew what was coming; their father was not necessarily an unkind man. But he held certain beliefs rather strongly. Children being allowed to sneak out to investigate a murder in the dead of night was not one of them.

‘So, what’s the play here, mmm? Laura?’ Johnno asked. Usually he would have been furious at her, but after everything they had seen.

‘I shouldn’t have told him we were investigating.’ She sighed, ‘That was stupid.’

‘A man is missing; what else were we supposed to do?’

‘So what do we say to him? Do we just tell him the truth? We were bird watching. And then we saw Ty Senior acting strangely.’ She sighed again. ‘And then, he just disappeared, we called Dad because we were worried about him.’ She finished.

‘I thought you said you already told him we were investigating Ty Senior.’

‘No. I told him we were investigating, but I didn’t tell him what we were investigating. You can investigate birds, right?’

‘Well, I guess. Seems pretty thin. I suppose I did get a shot of the Eastern Barn.’

‘Good. Okay. I mean, really, he should be thanking us. We didn’t have to call this in.’

He hesitated for a moment. ‘Let’s say he doesn’t kill us over this. What do we say about—’ he pointed toward the water, ‘—that stuff.

‘We say nothing,’ Laura said firmly. ‘He wouldn’t be able to comprehend it anyway.’

‘You better hide your recorder then.’ She stuffed it into her sock as a pair of yellow headlights rounded the bend and bounced towards them. ‘Crap. Here he comes.’


Chapter 5

It took maybe a minute for Sergeant Dick Harris to arrive at the clearing, trampling his way through the bush with a sour look on his face. He shone his torch directly at them, making Laura squint and look away. ‘Well,’ he said. ‘If it isn’t Watson and Sherlock. Far from their consulting rooms in Baker Street.’ He pronounced it rooms, in that hard, round way.

‘Dad—’ Laura started.

‘Please, Laura, just give me the recorder.’

Johnno opened his mouth as if to say something. But Dick held up his finger at him. ‘Ah. Tape Recorder. Now.’

‘I don’t have it. I left it at home.’ Laura said, mustering all the sincerity she could.

Dick gritted his teeth into a now psychotic grin. ‘Somehow I doubt that.’

‘No, really,’ Johnno interjected. ‘When she said we were investigating. You see, it was my idea. What she really meant to say was that we were investigating birds.’ He held up his camera. ‘See.’

‘Birds?’ His grin was getting thinner, stretching his mouth wider and wider.

‘Yes. An Eastern Barn Owl. Very rare.’ He finished lamely.

Dick didn’t say anything, thinking everything over. He leaned toward them, then said very quietly, enunciating every word, ‘Then why. Did. You. Radio me?’

Laura spoke this time. ‘Well, as we mentioned, we were investigating, these birds—well, really, I should have said that we were just watching them, because you don’t really investigate birds, do you?’ She laughed nervously, plunging on. ‘And you see, we saw that there was someone, a man, on the Estuary. Shouting and carrying on.’

‘We thought he was maybe drunk, making a lot of noise.’ Johnno added.

‘Yes, we thought he was drunk. And you see, well, we were worried about him. So we, we followed him for a bit and, we got distracted, to tell you the truth—’

‘By the birds?’ Offered Dick.

‘Yes, exactly. By the birds. There was this great, big bird, shrieking and shrieking. And when we looked back, there was a splash, and, well, the man was gone. Which is when I knew I should radio you, because, well. You’re a police officer of course. And I was worried that, maybe he had drowned—.’

‘You don’t seem that worried to me,’ interrupted Dick.

‘Oh, well, no. I mean. I’m worried. We should be looking for him right now.’ She said.

‘Did you recognise the man by any chance?’ He asked.

Laura shot a look at Johnno. ‘No. He was too far away. Anyway, we weren’t really looking at him We’re more, concerned citizens than active participants.’

‘Hm. Concerned citizens.’ Whether or not he believed them, it wasn’t clear. He swung his torch away from their faces and toward the estuary. ‘You better show me where he was then.’ They hurried over toward the place Ty Senior had been fishing.

Laura pointed to now empty boat, gently drifting in the water. ‘There’s his boat.’

Dick squinted at the scene before him. ‘Looks like his boat alright. No serious water man leaves his boat unattended mind you.’ He stared at them. Still unsure what to make of the situation. Their faces now tight from tiredness, now waiting for the hammer the fall.. ‘The fact you snuck out of the house without my permission notwithstanding. Let’s say I believe you.’

They let out a slow collective breath of relief, so as to make sure he couldn’t hear. ‘Shall we help you look for him then?’ Laura looked at him. ‘That would be the next logical step, right? Make sure he’s not close by?’

Dick smiled at her gently. ‘No, no, no. He’ll be long gone now, wherever he is. Besides, we won’t find anything useful till sun up. And you two are my prime suspects in a missing persons case. Best thing for you is to think over what you’ve done.’ His smile looked less gentle now. ‘You of course, have the right to remain silent. Etcetera, etcetera.’ He chuckled and opened his cuffs with a flick of his wrist, slapping a collar arround each of their wrists.

‘Dad. Are you serious?’ Johnno looked unsure of what he was supposed to do.

‘I’m serious. Grab your bag, let’s go.’

To any casual observer watching them bounce through the scrub, they might have looked like an extremely unusual caterpillar. Dick prodding at their backs as they worked themselves into the carrier. Although, if you looked carefully, you might have sworn you could see the glimmer of a smile in his eyes as he floored the accelerator, sirens blaring, toward town.


Chapter 6

Johnno rubbed his wrist where the cuff had cut into it, watching Laura pace the small concrete cell, muttering to herself.

‘Ridiculous. I mean, we’re his children… this is some kind of sick joke.’

He rubbed his eyes. The sun now shone brightly through the bars on the window. ‘Would you give it a rest for a second? He’s not coming back for a while, all right. He’ll let us out eventually.’

She spun round to face him. ‘We’ve been in here for hours. What else do you expect me to do?’

‘I don’t know. Help me figure out what the hell is going on?’

She snorted. ‘We’re as in the dark as anyone. More so, being stuck in here.’ Starting her pacing again.

‘You don’t know that.’ He reasoned. ‘He’s more likely punishing us for sneaking out than actually treating this as a case. Daniel Ty was the bottom of the social barrel. I don’t think he’ll be much missed.’

‘Don’t be so horrible.’ She paused, mid-pace. ‘Let’s review the facts.’

‘Wait.’ Johnno stuck his ear through the bars, listening for any sound from inside the station. ‘I think we’re good.’

Laura already had the recorder out of her sock. ‘Detective Harris here; it’s approximately 10 am. I’m at the Dawesville Station, in a cell with my partner. Reviewing the suspect’s disappearance, Daniel Ty was acting strangely on the estuary last night.’ She paused for a moment. ‘Why, exactly, we don’t know. Although we have evidence that he was putting something, possibly remains, wrapped in black plastic, inside several of the crabbing pots. Which we suspect do not belong to him.’ She paused again. ‘What happened next is a little hard to describe. While my partner and I were distracted—’ Johnno shot her a guilty glance. ‘—we realised the suspect had vanished. I use the word vanished because there was no evidence of foul play or a planned escape. While searching for him, a large splash caught our attention, which led us to discover several—at least fifty—dead fish in the shallows of the southern edge of the estuary. Best as we could tell, they died from a lack of water. Possibly in relation to the splash. Their bodies had been sucked dry, like they had been in a dehydrator.’ She clicked the recorder off.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Finally, Johnno broke the silence. ‘It’s a little hard to describe alright.’

‘It’s fucking weird is what it is. None of it makes any sense. It feels like the start of some terrible horror film.’

‘Maybe it is.’ He laughed dryly. ‘Although there was something about that splash that looked familiar. Do you remember last summer, when they made us go fishing in biology? And we spotted those whales lobtailing off the coast while the fish we caught were flapping around us on the jetty?’

‘Mmm. I do.’ She thought for a moment. ‘But how does a whale end up in the estuary? And what does it have to do with dehydrated fish?’

‘I don’t know.’ He rubbed his eyes again. ‘We’re missing something. We need more information about Daniel, who he is, what he might have been doing, or what he was involved in. I remember another name from the Stanton file. A connection. Someone the never spoke to.’

‘You mean his son? Kayden? He was a few years above us at school. But they’re estranged anyway.’

‘Better than nothing, don’t you think?’

‘I suppose. If Dad ever lets us out of here, that is.’ She sighed, resuming her pacing.


Chapter 7

Dick was thankful Officer Lee was out in the water and not him; the estuary could be freezing this time of year. Not to mention a man had just gone missing. Well, maybe. He caught her signal and started the winch to reel the boat in. He thought about what the kids had said; the small voice in the back of his head whispered: if you can’t see the bottom, you don’t know what’s down there. He shook his head.

‘Easy does it!’ He heard the boat nose the sand as Lee jumped from the bow into the scrub. ‘She’s home.’

He walked over to where she stood, beaming at him. ‘Good. I’m going to take a look. Catalogue what you find. You can take it to the impound after.’

‘No worries, Boss.’

Lee was always cheerful, although Dick often reminded her that being a little grumpy made you a better cop. You’ve got to protect your peace, was his favourite phrase to use around her. He took his pen from his pocket and leaned over the gunwale to get a proper look inside. Nothing in particular looked out of place. A few beer cans, cigarette butts, fishing gear. I mean, who didn’t drink while fishing? It was piss boring. He poked around the pile of bait and tackle, lifting a scrunched-up jumper to reveal several tightly wrapped black plastic bags underneath.

Lee was watching him work. ‘What’s that, Boss?’

He didn’t say anything, just grunted softly as he unwound one of the bags. The smell hit him instantly. He recoiled backwards, struggling not to gag as his eyes filled with tears. ‘Jesus, fuck.’

Lee wasn’t much better. She was doubled over with her hand over her nose. ‘Smells like my mother-in-law’s cooking.’

Dick stole one last breath of fresh air, then risked a look inside the bag. There were three hoofs—half-rotted and covered in maggots—inside. ‘Cow feet!’ He called over to Lee.

‘What do you think he was trying to catch with that?’

‘No idea.’ He muttered, looking back in the bag. The little voice in his head was back: you don’t know what’s down there… he shook the bag gently until he had got a good look inside, then cursed again. On the ankle of one of the hoofs was a small branding mark. A capitalised G, followed by a flying, winged F. He knew immediately what it meant. Everyone knew GF stood for: ‘This belongs to Guinness Farms, so you’d best stay away unless you want to find out what a shotgun feels like when it’s pressed into your back.’ He wiped his brow, thinking fast. He didn’t particularly want to involve the Guinnesses, but what choice did he have? It was better than the alternative, or, god forbid, the whole thing ending up in the papers. He sighed, closed the bag and bundled the rest of them up. ‘All right, I’m going to follow these up. Leave them off the catalogue, will you?’ He tried to sound casual.

She looked at him quizzically for a moment, as if wanting to ask him more, but decided against it. ‘No problem, as long as I don’t have to smell that any more, I’m happy.’

He nodded, then added before leaving. ‘Radio me if you find anything else.’


Chapter 8

The beast wasn’t yet afraid, but it knew something was wrong. He felt its neck bulge as he repositioned his grip on the sticking knife pressed against its flesh. He went in slowly at first. Then, as the first cries started and his hand began to feel warm from the blood, he finished it quickly before it started to thrash. He stepped back, watching the life slip from its eyes, admiring the line of now dead cattle draining themselves in the courtyard, their blood forming a small river that slipped easily toward the central gutter, which, mixed with the litres of shit and pissed-out animal feed, sloshed its way merrily toward the river at the bottom of the property.

Trenton Guinness looked up from his work as a car crunched to a stop on the gravel driveway in front of the house. A man in a police uniform climbed out, carrying a stack of black plastic bags. He stared. Watched him for a moment, wiped his hands on his plastic apron and wandered over. ‘Can I help you?’

The man looked uncomfortable, like he’d been here once before and had hoped he wouldn’t be back. ‘Is your grandfather in?’

‘Should be. I’ll take you to his office.’ Trenton wasn’t surprised the man knew of him. He was used to it by now, given who his grandfather was. They headed toward the house. It was a Federation-era double-storey limestone construction. The colloquial name was still Guinness Farmhouse, but it was more estate than farm. They had over 1,000 hectares and nearly 5,000 head of cattle, sheep, & pigs, plus a newly built state-of-the-art electronic milking shed at the east of the property. As they crossed the stained-glass entry, Trenton slipped off his boots. ‘Shoes off, please.’ As he turned to check that the officer had done the same, he saw he already had.

They climbed the curved, polished jarrah stairs together, heading to the second floor. At the end of a long corridor lined with windows looking over the expanse of the farm, he knocked twice.

A thin voice came from inside. ‘Come in.’

The man inside was crumpled with age. He could have been easily over one hundred years old, except for his smile, which danced across his face as they entered the room. Trenton spoke. ‘Hello, grandfather. Man here to see you. Officer, what was it again?’

‘We’ve met before. Sergeant Harris. Dick Harris.’ The officer shook hands with the old man.

‘Of course. Mr. Harris. Good to see you again. Thomas Peel. Well, I suppose you’d already know that.’

* * *

The door clicked shut as the boy stepped from the room. Dick cleared his throat. The old man’s grin widened. ‘Nice boy, isn’t he?’

‘If you say so.’

‘He’s only seventeen, you know. Already half-running the cattle operation.’

‘I saw. As long as it’s just cattle he’s slaughtering.’

Thomas Peel chuckled softly to himself. ‘Death is a natural part of life. Where do you get your meat from, Sergeant?’

Dick considered this for a moment. He tossed the bags onto the desk, wrinkling his nose as the smell slowly seeped out.

Thomas Peel didn’t seem to notice the smell, but leaned forward, halfsurprised. ‘Well, well. Where did you get these from?’

‘Found them inside Ty Senior’s boat. Your branding mark on them.’ He didn’t need to say it; the old man clearly knew what they were.

Thomas Peel furrowed his brow. He grinned again. ‘What a man does with his own stock is his own business. We’re all entitled to that at least.’

‘So he was working for you?’

‘That’s right. We like to give fellas a second chance round here.’

Dick paused for a moment. Then, as casually as he could, ‘Your “fella” has been reported missing from the estuary. Vanished into thin air last night. Left his gear and everything just floating there.’

‘That so? At Guinness Farms, we take the safety of our workers very seriously.’

Neither of them said anything for a second. ‘I know your family means a lot to this community. But you’ve got to help me out here Thomas. What was it for? And don’t tell me it was fish bait.’

Thomas Peel shifted in his chair, then looked Dick straight in the eyes. ‘Let’s put it this way. Hows about I pass on my generous thanks to the police department for the safe return of Ty Senior’s boat. And you let me know if you find anything else that might help us bring him home.’ He opened a drawer and pulled out a small chequebook. ‘How does, say, ten thousand sound? As a reward, of course.’

Dick suppressed a cough, looking away. That was almost half his salary. And significantly more than last time. He had turned it down then. Keeping the community safe is reward enough for me. He could feel Thomas Peel’s eyes on him. Back then, his wife had still been around—money was tighter now—not to mention all the bloody things kids demanded from you these days. He met the grinning eyes again. ‘You can make it out to me. One of your boys can pick up the boat from the impound tonight.’


Chapter 9

Thomas Peel watched the Sergeant walk back to his car. Filthy meddling shit stain of a policeman. A Brit, no less. Well, he took the bribe. There was that, at least. He cracked his knuckles on his desk, gently stroking the thin line of moustache on his upper lip.

The door opened. A woman walked in; it was Anne. Beautiful Anne. She walked over to him and wrapped her arms around his shoulders, gently placing a kiss on the top of his head.

‘Hello Daddy.’

He smiled at the word, rubbing her arms. ‘Hello darling.’

‘What did the man want?’

‘Just returning some things of ours.’ He motioned to the bags on the table.

‘I see. And what did you do about it?’

‘He won’t be talking about it anytime soon.’

‘You gave him money? Or threatened him.’

’Money.’

‘His wife died under mysterious circumstances in England a few years back.’ He tensed under her. ‘Sorry. Just in case we need more.’

He relaxed. He was getting sentimental with age. Anne was, of course, his only remaining child. Nine deaths, even if some of them had been in-laws, had taken their toll on him. He remembered the first two. A car crash on the state highway; it had been his first day as Mayor. He had only been thirty-six, his secretary delivering the news, tears streaming down her face. Everyone had adored Jill and Edward. His only son, snatched from him. Wayne had been next. Anne’s husband. A strong man. His heart exploded while laying roofing sheets on a forty-degree day in December; it had been around Christmas time. The postman saw him tumble from the roof and thud into the pavement. Darling Linda, who had been his favourite, and Brian had been killed in a terrible accident—their helicopter had gone down while herding cattle five years back. Brian had been at the controls, their two children in the back. Suddenly, four little ones had been left at home without parents or grandparents to care for them. Anne had taken them in for a time, naturally. No matter what the town said about him, money and power couldn’t buy everything. So many still relied on him—four remaining grandchildren and twelve great-grandchildren. He looked up at Anne. ‘Let’s hope it doesn’t come to that.’

She nodded. ‘And Daniel? What happened to him? He never reported back last night, probably drunk at a bar by now, mind you.’

‘He was reported missing this morning. That’s what started this whole problem.’

‘Hm. I see. Do we know who reported it? I’d like to have a word with them.’

‘Not yet.’

‘See if you can find out. You should have asked him.’

Thomas clicked his tongue impatiently. ‘We can’t have him thinking anything is wrong.’

She took a breath; clearly, she didn’t agree. ‘If you say so. I suppose panic is the last thing we need now. Besides, it didn’t take the bait; maybe it left the estuary. Do you think we loop Bryce in yet? Don’t you think he ought to know what’s been going on?’

‘No. Keep him out of it for now. We need him to be clean. He had enough to worry about. Being Mayor isn’t all sunshine and rainbows my dear.’

‘Fine.’ She straightened herself. ‘I’ll send Tristan up to get rid of those bags. They smell awful.’

He cackled to himself as she turned and left.


Chapter 10

As the troopie bounced back onto the main road, Dick watched the farmhouse on the hill slowly recede in his mirror. He let out a long breath. Ten thousand dollars. That would go a long way, to be sure. He fought the tightening in his chest as he thought of what he had given up to get it. A gift like that in his line of work was not unusual, in-fact it was almost expected, a perk of the job. But still. The Peel family weren’t ones to be trifled with, especially since his own kids had reported Ty Senior missing. But the money was for them in the end. The first rule of family is never turn on one of your own. He was now one of them. The money would protect the kids, not harm them. Besides, no one ever said he had to cash the cheque straight away. The only question was, what more did he owe them?

He turned back toward town, thinking over last night’s events. Nothing yet made sense. What had those bags been for? What did Ty Senior have to do with it all? Where had he vanished to? And why specifically had Thomas Peel asked him to stop looking into it? The questions went round and round in his head. But no answer came from it.

The dash radio warbled at him. He grabbed the receiver. ‘Go for Harris.’

‘Hi boss, it’s me. Boats in the impound, just like you asked.’

‘Good. Someone will be over to pick it up this afternoon.’

‘So we’re stopping the search?’

He paused for a moment. ‘I asked around. Apparently Ty Senior’s been spotted at a pub down toward Harvey. No mystery, just forgot to tie up his boat.’

‘What about what your kids said?’

‘They imagined the whole thing. Too many detective shows can warp a kid’s brain.’

‘You can say that again. Those shows are too scary for me.’

He grunted. ‘Did you find anything else on the boat?’

‘Mmm, let me check my notes.’ She dropped off the air for a second. ‘Not really. Just the usual things. Lifejackets, etcetera.’ Dick sighed a breath of relief. ‘Oh yeah, there was one thing though. On the bottom of the boat, I noticed a few deep scratches in the hull. About 5 millimetres deep, and maybe a metre long? No idea what it’s from; looked fairly recent.’

Dick felt his chest tighten again. ‘Probably just ran into the reef or something.’

‘Maybe. Although it looked more like an animal, like when my dog scratches at the door at night.’

If you can’t see the bottom, you don’t know what’s down there. The road roared in Dick’s ears. They could be anything down there. He gripped the wheel tighter with his free hand.

‘But I guess giant dogs don’t live in the Dawesville Estuary, right?’

‘No.’

‘Oh, and one other strange thing. The taps in the station aren’t working. Nothing comes out when I turn any of them on. It’s like the water’s run dry or something.’

‘Call the plumber; use the petty cash. Someone must have turned the main line off down the road.’ He shook his head. That one she was going to have to figure out for herself. ‘Oh, and before I forget. Let the kids out, will you? Let’s hope they’ve learnt their lesson.’

‘Don’t want to think what might happen if they haven’t! They’re good kids Dick. I know it’s not always easy, but just like my mum used to say to me: “Good kids don’t just go bad. They just need a bit of love to remember they were always good.”’

He clicked off the receiver. She was right; he promised himself he would hug them tonight. He shuddered, thinking of the scratches Lee found in the boat. It must have come from the reef. To think anything else was ridiculous. To think anything else was to think things he didn’t want to think. Of darkness, of mud, of fear. He stepped on the gas, pushing the thought from his mind.


Chapter 11

Laura stood at the start of the jetty. It was at least fifteen meters long and made with driftwood, nailed together. She turned toward Johnno. ‘Do you think it’s legal?’

‘Do you think it’s safe?’

At the end of the jetty sat what looked like a makeshift floating greenhouse. It was no more than three meters wide and six meters long, held together with angle iron and roped-together poly drums. They could see a figure, in shadow, watching them from inside. ‘Come on, let’s go.’

They walked carefully down the jetty, single file, then knocked three times on the door of the houseboat. The figure didn’t move….
