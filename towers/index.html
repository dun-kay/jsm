Read the Towers blurb...


In the spring of 1994, something terrible has awakened in Dawesville, Australia.

Fish are dying in the estuary, and strange disappearances are plaguing the usually tranquil small town.

When the younger sister of local 'it' girl Mary Macy vanishes, amateur detective Laura Harris and her reluctant twin brother, Johnno, finally have the case they've been waiting for.

As the town's disappearances mount, their unlikely group only grows. Mary joins them, desperate to find her sister. So does Kayden Ty, searching for his missing father.

But some things, once taken by the estuary, don't easily come back. And what's out there in the depths didn't wake up on its own.

Stephen King's small-town dread meets Twin Peaks' surreal menace — with a twist of dry Australian wit. Towers is the first instalment in a mystery series that will make you afraid of the water, and those that lurk around it... forever.

Chapter 1

The creature—for that’s all he could think to call it—was humming a tune. At least that’s what it sounded like. That was the last thing he remembered as the water filled his ears and his shoulder hit the warm muck and darkness that lay on the estuary bed.

Chapter 2

‘Jesus Laura, will you please shut up?’ She almost ran into Johnno as he stopped dead and swung round to face her. The tape recorder in her hand whirred to a stop as the last magnetic particles wrote themselves into the reel.

‘What’s your problem?’

‘You’re not Dale Cooper—’

‘FBI Special Agent Dale Cooper,’ she corrected him.

‘You’re not FBI Special Agent Dale Cooper. This isn’t some TV show; we’re not in Twin Peaks. This is Dawesville, real life. If Ty Senior catches us following him, we’re going to be in serious trouble, all right?’

She knew Johnno had a point. If Ty Senior had any reputation around town, it wasn’t a good one. Although seeing his red hair plastered against his pale forehead, the whites of his eyes popping in the moonlight… she decided she didn’t care. She grinned, then extended her free hand to him.

‘What?’ Johnno stared.

‘You can hold my hand if you like.’

He kicked the ground with his boot. ‘I’m not scared all right, it’s just that—’

She didn’t wait for him to finish, hiding her smirk as she pushed a low-hanging branch out of the way and continued towards the darkly glistening water, raising the tape recorder back to her mouth as she went, ‘Detective Laura Harris resuming her report. It’s nearly midnight on the first of September 1994. Conditions on the southern edge of the Dawesville Estuary are clear, a full moon providing good visibility. We are currently pursuing Daniel Ty, the suspect—’

She heard Johnno pick his way toward where she was crouched, hidden behind a small bank of reeds on the water’s edge. He hissed in her ear as he placed his bag carefully onto the sand, ‘Will you at least whisper?’

She lowered her voice. ‘Who is approximately thirty meters offshore and appears to be fishing. Although the navigation lights on his boat are switched off, which is, naturally, suspicious.’

Johnno snorted, ‘There’s no way he’s out here just fishing. I’m ninety-nine per cent sure that man over there murdered Andrew Stanton. Ty Senior was the last person seen with him. I mean seriously, what more do you need?’

Laura frowned at him. Yes, Andrew Stanton had gone missing. Yes, Ty Senior was the prime suspect, but the police report had specifically noted a lack of hard evidence. ‘Well, we’ll still need something to connect him to it. And ideally it’ll come in the form of a body.’

Johnno clicked his tongue and reached into his bag, pulling out a pair of heavy binoculars for her. ‘Well, come on then.’ He took out his much-prized bird-watchers camera, and drew the camouflage hood tight around his head. ‘Let’s catch ourselves a god damn killer.’

Murderer or not, it was clear that Ty Senior worked with a practised hand; the whistle of his line arced across the estuary again and again, catching nothing more than dripping weed each time. This must have continued for at least an hour, because, before Laura could check her watch, Johnno nudged her and she realised her legs had gone numb and she had slipped half-asleep.

‘Get ready, looks like he’s about to move.’

Laura gently rocked side to side, coming out of her reverie—binoculars pressing into her cheeks as she squinted at the boat—cursing herself for drifting off. She watched him reach for the motor, his cigarette balanced in his mouth as he worked the starter cord, lifting his cap to wipe the sweat from his brow as the engine finally spluttered to life.

‘What’s he doing?’ Johnno breathed. ‘Has he seen us?’

‘Shut up.’ She hissed back at him. Ty Senior was easing the boat directly toward where they were hidden. He cut the engine and let the boat glide for a moment, then, in one swift movement, grabbed the gaff from its holder on the gunwale and leaned over the bow to catch a line of rope floating just below the surface. He was maybe ten meters away now, close enough that Laura didn’t need the binoculars to make out what was happening. A largeish mesh cage, the size of a carry-on suitcase, thunked against the side of the boat—she could see now it was part of a series of ten or so buoys—as he hauled it aboard. Water poured from it, pooling on the deck. ‘Crab pots. He was banned from crabbing last year but.’

‘I know, it was in the Herald.’ Johnno whispered back, clicking off several photos. They watched him, his back to them, lift the mesh flap of the first pot and push something inside, wrapped tightly in black plastic.

Laura glanced at Johnno, almost sure they were having the same thought. Is that… chopped-up bits of Andrew Stanton? She nudged him. ‘We need to get a look in one of those pots’. He shot back a look to ask if she was crazy. ‘Think about it, the estuary isn’t actually that deep here. We can come back as soon as it’s light with a mask and dive weights.’ He opened his mouth to reply, but before he could, a terrible scream twisted out across the water. It came again, louder this time, and directly behind them. Laura spun around, careful not to give away their position, scanning the dark bush behind her, every shape and shadow now a threat.

A few moments passed. Johnno, panting with worry, struggled to get the words out. ‘I don’t see. Anyone.’

As if in response, a pale shape burst from the canopy and swept directly toward them. They flattened themselves into the ground, as Johnno let out a laugh of relief. ‘It’s an owl. I can’t believe I didn’t recognise the call.’

‘Careful, quiet.’ Laura hissed.

‘Sorry. They’re really rare, you know,’ he whispered, following the gliding shape with his camera, watching it as it reached the opposite bank of the estuary and landed on a branch with a soft flurry of its wings. ‘Beautiful.’

Laura looked back at the boat—her heart slowly thumping back to normal—then cursed. At what point it had happened, she wasn’t sure, and whether it had been done deliberately or not, she didn’t know; but Daniel Ty was gone. She stood up, throwing all pretence of stealth out the window. ‘Where did he go? We looked away for maybe thirty seconds!’

‘Do you think he swam to shore?’ Johnno asked, eyeing the water nervously. ‘Maybe we should get out of here.’

‘No, wait here. If he comes for us, just start swinging or something.’ She swept the binoculars across the water again, muttering to herself. ‘Come on… come on… where are you?’ The boat was still there, the gaff leaning across the gunwale, a thin curl of smoke rising from the cigarette balanced in the tab of an open beer can. Laura lowered the binoculars. ‘If you were going to slip away from someone. Do you think you’d finish your cigarette first?’

Johnno looked over. ‘Probably. Out of habit, I guess.’

‘Look at this.’

Johnno scanned the scene, taking a beat to gather his thoughts. ‘People don’t just vanish into thin air. I mean, he must have gone somewhere.’

A moment later, a splash exploded to their right. Laura only just caught it out of the corner of her eye: a giant column of water, at least three meters tall. It hung in the air for a moment, then crashed down. ‘Go!’ She shouted.

Johnno was already ahead of her, sprinting through the scrub that lined the water’s edge towards the place where the column had been, the camera bouncing wildly from the strap around his neck. They skidded to a halt. Johnno, with his hands on his knees, panted, ‘What was that?’

Blinking the last white dots of exertion from her eyes, she carefully approached the edge of the water. It was slack now, glassy enough for her to peer into the shallows. She caught herself at the last second, reeling back as a hundred eyes stared back at her. ‘Fish,’ she called. ‘Dead fish. Hundreds of them.’ They looked silver in the moonlight, no more than ten centimetres long and five wide. Nothing natural could have done that; the scene was as odd as it was terrible. Thinking back, she wasn’t sure if it was the terror or the strangeness that made her do what she did next: grimacing as the icy water flooded her boots.

‘Careful!’ Johnno cautioned. ‘Laura. Please. Don’t go any deeper.’

‘Don’t worry, they can’t die twice.’ She called, reaching into the water and gingerly picking one up. It felt hard in her hand. Like it had been lying in the sun for many days. She tossed it toward Johnno. ‘Get a photo of that.’

His hand shook slightly as he placed it in the shot as a reference for size. ‘Do you think whatever did this also…’ he trailed off.

Laura searched around the area where the fish lay, the water turning a milky white. Her brow was furrowed as she looked for any evidence or reason to explain what they had just seen. A flash of red caught her eye. She toed the sand where she had seen it. It was a cap—bright red. Johnno took another photograph, his jaw clenching and unclenching. He recognised it immediately.

Laura raised the tape recorder to her lips, her finger over the record button. After a moment, she lowered it again and looked directly at Johnno. ‘I think we need to call Dad.’

Chapter 3

Dick Harris didn’t particularly like working the night shift at the station. It wasn’t the lack of sleep that bothered him; as a Sergeant, he had long since learnt the trick to staying alert—although his children always complained the next day that it made his breath smell like rotting cardboard. It wasn’t even the odd calls he had to take that bothered him—a drunken couple fighting, reports of lost dogs (that he would usually find sometime later, dead on the highway), or some depressed bastard who had drunk too much of something and wandered into the bush. He sighed, tilting his hat further over his eyes. No, it was the god-damn lighting that bothered him most. The new fluorescent tubes they had installed in the tiny office they called Dawesville Station made him feel sick. He hoped they would soon centralise operations back to the City of Mandurah. He spread his fingers wide and placed his palms on either side of the report he was trying to edit, willing himself back to focus.

The car was stolen at approximately nine-thirty on Tuesday morning. The suspect, male, mid-thirties, in a dark tracksuit jumper—he stared at his hands; his skin looked half green, like it had been dipped in dog piss. He shook his head, glancing around for a distraction. No wonder everyone hated cops; we’re all pissed off all the time. He groaned and eased himself out of his chair. Mid-upward stretch, the radio alarm warbled at him from across the station. He glanced down at his wrist—the numbers on his watch glowed one-thirty. Who on earth could be calling at this time? **He walked toward the receiver and snatched it from its cradle.

‘Harris.’ He barked. There was nothing but static on the other end. Then, a voice, breathless and very small, crackled through.

‘Dad?’

‘Laura? What’s wrong?’ His voice turned to worry almost immediately. She didn’t say anything for at least five full seconds before he broke the silence. ‘Are you in trouble?’

‘No. Not exactly.’

‘Elaborate.’ His initial concern evaporating.

‘We’re at the estuary, on the southern side. We were investigating—’

He didn’t wait to hear the rest. He barked a final order at her, ‘Stay there; I’m on my way.’ Then signed off. Grabbing his jacket from the hanger as he strode toward the 75’ Troop Carrier parked outside the station, the door banging close behind him.

The cool night air did little to calm him down. Muttering to himself as he pressed the accelerator, the car roaring under him. ‘Can’t believe… explicitly told them not to… investigating again.’ He swung off the main street and cut toward the estuary, picking up speed as he left town. He cursed David Lynch and Kyle McGlocklan, or whatever the fuck his name was. Ever since they had watched that stupid show last summer, it had been investigations this, disappearances that. I mean, seriously. What teenage kid spends their time casually researching murders? No. This was the limit. It was time to put his foot down. He swore. Damn it. **Whatever they had done. There would be no more after this. His brow now set, a cloud over his forehead. He counted the trunks of the trees rushing past, trying to calm himself down. Onetwothreefourfivesix… eightnineteneleven —thrusting his mind back to his childhood, his mother turning round to face him in the back seat.

‘Don’t worry darling, we’ll be home in no time. Just count the Wellingtonias, there’s a good boy. It’ll make you feel better I promise.’ His father chuckling as his mother rested her head against his broad shoulders, his Surry laugh carrying him to sleep before he realised they were back at the house.

What had he done wrong? He had been afraid of his parents, unable to disappoint them, yes, of course. That was how it was supposed to be. This country was certainly no England. Or maybe children these days were just different. They think they’re adults, like they have rights, opinions. Entitlements. Well, he thought, if that’s what they want—reaching down to flick on sirens on—that’s what they’ll get.

Chapter 4

Laura heard the sirens long before she saw them. Grimacing at Johnno as he looked up from where he was sitting on an upturned log, his head in his hands. They knew what was coming; their father was not necessarily an unkind man. But he held certain beliefs rather strongly. Children being allowed to sneak out to investigate a murder in the dead of night was not one of them.

‘So, what’s the play here, mmm? Laura?’ Johnno asked. Usually he would have been furious at her, but after everything they had seen.

‘I shouldn’t have told him we were investigating.’ She sighed, ‘That was stupid.’

‘A man is missing; what else were we supposed to do?’

‘So what do we say to him? Do we just tell him the truth? We were bird watching. And then we saw Ty Senior acting strangely.’ She sighed again. ‘And then, he just disappeared, we called Dad because we were worried about him.’ She finished.

‘I thought you said you already told him we were investigating Ty Senior.’

‘No. I told him we were investigating, but I didn’t tell him what we were investigating. You can investigate birds, right?’

‘Well, I guess. Seems pretty thin. I suppose I did get a shot of the Eastern Barn.’

‘Good. Okay. I mean, really, he should be thanking us. We didn’t have to call this in.’

He hesitated for a moment. ‘Let’s say he doesn’t kill us over this. What do we say about—’ he pointed toward the water, ‘—that stuff.

‘We say nothing,’ Laura said firmly. ‘He wouldn’t be able to comprehend it anyway.’

‘You better hide your recorder then.’ She stuffed it into her sock as a pair of yellow headlights rounded the bend and bounced towards them. ‘Crap. Here he comes.’

Chapter 5

It took maybe a minute for Sergeant Dick Harris to arrive at the clearing, trampling his way through the bush with a sour look on his face. He shone his torch directly at them, making Laura squint and look away. ‘Well,’ he said. ‘If it isn’t Watson and Sherlock. Far from their consulting rooms in Baker Street.’ He pronounced it rooms, in that hard, round way.

‘Dad—’ Laura started.

‘Please, Laura, just give me the recorder.’

Johnno opened his mouth as if to say something. But Dick held up his finger at him. ‘Ah. Tape Recorder. Now.’

‘I don’t have it. I left it at home.’ Laura said, mustering all the sincerity she could.

Dick gritted his teeth into a now psychotic grin. ‘Somehow I doubt that.’

‘No, really,’ Johnno interjected. ‘When she said we were investigating. You see, it was my idea. What she really meant to say was that we were investigating birds.’ He held up his camera. ‘See.’

‘Birds?’ His grin was getting thinner, stretching his mouth wider and wider.

‘Yes. An Eastern Barn Owl. Very rare.’ He finished lamely.

Dick didn’t say anything, thinking everything over. He leaned toward them, then said very quietly, enunciating every word, ‘Then why. Did. You. Radio me?’

Laura spoke this time. ‘Well, as we mentioned, we were investigating, these birds—well, really, I should have said that we were just watching them, because you don’t really investigate birds, do you?’ She laughed nervously, plunging on. ‘And you see, we saw that there was someone, a man, on the Estuary. Shouting and carrying on.’

‘We thought he was maybe drunk, making a lot of noise.’ Johnno added.

‘Yes, we thought he was drunk. And you see, well, we were worried about him. So we, we followed him for a bit and, we got distracted, to tell you the truth—’

‘By the birds?’ Offered Dick.

‘Yes, exactly. By the birds. There was this great, big bird, shrieking and shrieking. And when we looked back, there was a splash, and, well, the man was gone. Which is when I knew I should radio you, because, well. You’re a police officer of course. And I was worried that, maybe he had drowned—.’

‘You don’t seem that worried to me,’ interrupted Dick.

‘Oh, well, no. I mean. I’m worried. We should be looking for him right now.’ She said.

‘Did you recognise the man by any chance?’ He asked.

Laura shot a look at Johnno. ‘No. He was too far away. Anyway, we weren’t really looking at him We’re more, concerned citizens than active participants.’

‘Hm. Concerned citizens.’ Whether or not he believed them, it wasn’t clear. He swung his torch away from their faces and toward the estuary. ‘You better show me where he was then.’ They hurried over toward the place Ty Senior had been fishing.

Laura pointed to now empty boat, gently drifting in the water. ‘There’s his boat.’

Dick squinted at the scene before him. ‘Looks like his boat alright. No serious water man leaves his boat unattended mind you.’ He stared at them. Still unsure what to make of the situation. Their faces now tight from tiredness, now waiting for the hammer the fall.. ‘The fact you snuck out of the house without my permission notwithstanding. Let’s say I believe you.’

They let out a slow collective breath of relief, so as to make sure he couldn’t hear. ‘Shall we help you look for him then?’ Laura looked at him. ‘That would be the next logical step, right? Make sure he’s not close by?’

Dick smiled at her gently. ‘No, no, no. He’ll be long gone now, wherever he is. Besides, we won’t find anything useful till sun up. And you two are my prime suspects in a missing persons case. Best thing for you is to think over what you’ve done.’ His smile looked less gentle now. ‘You of course, have the right to remain silent. Etcetera, etcetera.’ He chuckled and opened his cuffs with a flick of his wrist, slapping a collar arround each of their wrists.

‘Dad. Are you serious?’ Johnno looked unsure of what he was supposed to do.

‘I’m serious. Grab your bag, let’s go.’

To any casual observer watching them bounce through the scrub, they might have looked like an extremely unusual caterpillar. Dick prodding at their backs as they worked themselves into the carrier. Although, if you looked carefully, you might have sworn you could see the glimmer of a smile in his eyes as he floored the accelerator, sirens blaring, toward town.
