Chapter 1

Daniel Ty was sure he was not dead. At least, he was quite sure of it. Because how could you feel angry if you were dead? He had lost track of exactly how he had ended up in his current situation, but as he awoke to his senses, he noticed that it was very dark. Well, to be more precise. There was exactly no light at all. And that he was neither cold nor warm. Thinking back, he couldn’t remember why he felt so angry.

Still, there was something tugging him toward a deep recess in his brain. Like a radio playing as he was falling asleep—the words drifting towards him through the darkness, ‘sleep no more, sleep is dead, sleep no more on the estuary bed’. A strange choice given his current circumstance; he hadn’t listened to The Triffids for—it must have been at least ten years. The reality slammed into his half-conscious brain hard and quite suddenly. Estuary. Bed. Triffid. Yes, he remembered now. He had been fishing, of course, in the Estuary—but why? The question waited for him in the blackness. Ah, yes. Of course. The anger became a beacon; then, the memory now fully formed—the snarling taxidermied face of a bull-sized male Red Kangaroo.

He remembered remarking to himself, gazing up at it hanging above the desk at the Peel Family Farm, that it looked remarkably like the crumpled face of the old man who sat in front of him. He wondered if they had fixed it that way deliberately. Despite his great age—being ninety was of course no mean feat—Thomas Peel was as vicious as ever. ‘So, are we agreed?’ Daniel nodded numbly, looking down at the slab Jarrah table in front of him—the tree it had come from must have been at least three times his age, a single piece as thick as half his hand. He wondered what they had thought when it fell. The thin, nasal bark of Thomas Peel brought him back to reality. ‘Nasty crabbers… I want them dealt with… good man.’ The word nasty had bothered him particularly. He wondered what the crabbers would think of him now. They had once been friends, or at least those you nodded at stoically on your way into the bar. The memory faded, his current blackness returning. He sighed. No, the last years had not been kind to him. Although there was a kind of bitter irony, a rage, that even though his family had been amongst those to first live in the area, he was doing the bidding of those who owned it now.

He supposed whatever he had coming, he deserved. He thought of his Son and Wife—they had, of course, both long since departed from his life. He wondered where they were now. He doubted they would even recognise him. He ran his hands over his face, his fingers coming to rest, splayed, in the nook between the edge of his nose and the start of his cheekbones. He was surprised at how soft it felt—despite the thick lines of age that hung there. Must all old men feel this way? He thought of his childhood, his school shoes slung over his shoulder, the sand rough between his toes, another red sunset dancing off the shining wetness that was left by the not-quite-dried receding tide. He closed his eyes, living in those moments one last time, humming the final lines of the song to himself, ‘ache no more, old skin is shed, sleep no more on the estuary bed’.

Chapter 2

‘Jesus Laura, will you shut up for once?’ She skidded to a halt, almost running into Johnno as he stopped dead and swung round to face her. The tape recorder in her hand whirred to a stop as the last magnetic particles, now carrying just static, wrote themselves onto the tiny reel inside the sleek plastic casing. She had been mid-explanation, adding an important entry into the case files—

‘What is your problem?’

‘You’re not Dale Cooper—’

‘FBI Special Agent Dale Cooper,’ she corrected him.

‘FBI Special Agent Dale Cooper. This isn’t some TV show; we’re not in Twin Peaks. This is Dawesville, real life. If Ty Senior catches us following him, we’re going to be in serious trouble all right?’

She knew Johnno had a point. If Ty Senior had any reputation around town, it wasn’t a good one. Although seeing his red hair plastered against his pale forehead, the whites of his eyes catching in the moonlight, she decided she didn’t care. She grinned, then extended her free hand to him.

‘What?’ Johnno stared.

‘I get it. You’re scared. You can hold my hand if you like—’

He kicked the ground with his boot, looking down. ‘I’m not scared all right, it’s just that—’

She didn’t wait for him to finish, hiding her smirk as she pushed a low-hanging paperbark branch out of the way and continued towards the darkly glistening water ahead of her, raising the tape recorder back to her mouth as she went, ‘Detective Laura Harris resuming her report. It’s nearly midnight on the first of September 1994. Conditions on the southern edge of the Dawesville Estuary are clear, with a full moon providing excellent visibility. We are currently pursuing the suspect, Mr Daniel Ty—’

She heard Johnno pick his way toward where she was crouched—hidden behind a small bank of reeds on the water’s edge. He hissed in her ear as he placed his bag carefully onto the sand, ‘Will you at least whisper?’

She lowered her voice. ‘—who is approximately thirty meters offshore and appears to be fishing.’

Johnno snorted, hissing back at her, ‘There’s no way he’s out here just fishing.’ She raised her eyebrows at him as he continued, ‘I’m ninety-nine per cent sure that man over there murdered Andrew Stanton. Ty Senior was the last person seen with him. I mean seriously, what more do you need?’

Laura grimaced. He seemed certain; however, she was more reluctant to accept the answer simply because it was obvious. Yes, Andrew Stanton had gone missing. Yes, Ty Senior was the prime suspect, but the police report had specifically noted a lack of hard evidence in the case. ‘Well, we’ll still need something to connect him to it. And ideally it’ll come in the form of a body.’ She muttered the last part, mostly for her own benefit.

Johnno clicked his tongue back at her and reached into his bag to pull out a pair of heavy binoculars that he handed to her. ‘Well, come on then, we’re late as it is.’ He hauled out his much-prized state-of-the-art EOS-1, and drew the camouflage hood tight around his head, as though preparing himself for what they might find. ‘Let’s catch ourselves a god damn killer then.’

Watching him in a careful silence, the never-ending staccato drone of crickets was punctuated only by the barely audible snap of the camera shutter. Murderer or not, it was clear that Ty Senior worked with a practised hand; the whistle of his line arcing across the estuary again and again—one, two, three, four—tugs, then he would reel it in. Except catching nothing more than dripping weed and algae each time, which he tossed back into the water with a wet slap, never showing any frustration or disappointment. This must have continued for at least an hour, because before Laura could check her watch, Johnno nudged her—her legs had long gone numb, and the cold had seeped through her jacket. She was also busting for a pee.

‘Get ready, looks like he’s about to move.’

Laura gently rocked herself side to side to ease the blood back into her legs—the binoculars pressing hard into her cheeks as she squinted at the figure on the boat. As he carefully placed the rod on the floor of the dinghy and reached for the motor, she could make out the yellow stains on his teeth—his cigarette balanced in his mouth in a mock grimace—as he worked the starter cord. The motor, finally spluttering to life, afforded him a long drag of celebration, his red cap, halved by a large gold lightning bolt, was lifted to wipe the sweat from his brow.

He eased the boat toward them, managing the throttle so it slid through the water, only creating as gentle a wake as was possible.

‘What’s he doing? Do you think he’s seen us?’ Johnno breathed.

‘Shut up and don’t move.’ It was her turn to hiss at him. It wasn’t immediately obvious what he was moving toward; his eyes seemed affixed on a spot just below the reeds they remained hidden behind, which now seemed impossibly thin and penetrable. He cut the engine and let the boat glide for a moment, then in one swift movement, grabbed a gaff from its holder on the gunwale and leaned over the bow to catch a line of rope floating just below the surface, hauling it into the boat in loose, wet coils. He was maybe ten meters away now, close enough that Laura didn’t need the binoculars to make out what was happening. A largeish mesh cage, the size of a carry-on suitcase, thunked against the side of the boat—she could see now it was part of a series of ten or so buoys. He reached down, fished out the first cage, and placed it on the deck to drain. ‘Crab pots. But they can’t be his. He was banned from crabbing last year—’

‘I remember; it was in the Herald.’ Johnno whispered back, clicking off several photos. They watched Ty Senior, his back to them now, lift the mesh flap of the first pot and push something inside wrapped tightly in black plastic. Laura glanced at Johnno, almost sure they were sharing the same thought.

You don’t think that’s bits of Andrew Stanton in there?

The sound of the pot hitting the water pulled her out of her reverie; it floated for a second, then was sucked back beneath the surface. She nudged Johnno. ‘We need to get a look in one of those pots’. He shot back a look, as if to ask if she were mad. ‘Think about it, the estuary isn’t actually that deep here. We can come back as soon as it’s light with a snorkel and dive weights and check the pots.’ He opened his mouth to reply, but a twisted scream echoed out across the water, interrupting him. It had a terrible, high-pitched, almost tortured quality. The scream came again, louder this time, and directly behind them. Laura spun around, careful not to give away their position, scanning the dark bush behind her, every shadow and shape a potential threat.

A few moments passed. Johnno, peeking from behind the camera, searched with her, almost panting with anticipation, struggling to get any words out. ‘I don’t see. Anyone.’

A pale shape burst from the canopy above them, sweeping down toward them and out across the water. Johnno laughed with relief, momentarily forgetting Ty Senior, the boat, and the bags. ‘It’s an eastern barn owl. I can’t believe I didn’t recognise the call.’

‘Shh! Stay quiet.’ Laura cautioned him.

‘Sorry. They’re really rare, you know,’ he whispered, following the gliding shape with his camera, watching it as it reached the opposite bank of the estuary and landed on a branch with a soft flurry of its wings. ‘Beautiful. What’s our man up to now? Enjoying the show as well?’

Laura looked back at the boat, then cursed. ‘Shit.’ At what point it had happened, she wasn’t sure, and whether it had been done deliberately or not, she didn’t know—but Daniel Ty was gone. She stood up, throwing all pretence of stealth out the window. ‘Shit. Where did he go? We looked away for maybe thirty seconds!’

‘Do you think he swam to shore?’ Johnno asked. Eying the dark shadows just beyond the trees again. ‘Maybe we should get out of here—’

‘No, wait right here. If he comes for us, just start swinging or something.’ She swept the binoculars across the water again, muttering to herself—‘come on… come on… where are you?’ Nothing looked immediately wrong. The boat was still there, the gaff leaning across the gunwale, the line dangling half-in the water, a thin curl of smoke rising from the cigarette balanced in the tab of the open beer can. In fact, she thought, the scene was almost too perfect. Staged. Frozen in time. Laura lowered the binoculars. ‘If you were going to slip away from someone. Do you think you’d finish your cigarette first?’

Johnno looked over. ‘Probably. Out of habit, if nothing else.’

‘Take a look at this.’

Johnno scanned the scene, taking a beat to gather his thoughts. ‘But people don’t just vanish into thin air. I mean, he must have gone somewhere.’

As if in response, a splash exploded to their right. Laura only just caught it out of the corner of her eye: a giant column of water, at least three meters tall—it hung in the air for a moment, then crashed down. ‘Go!’ She shouted, without meaning to say the words.

Johnno was already ahead of her, sprinting through the scrub that lined the water’s edge towards the place where the water still foamed, the camera bouncing wildly from the strap around his neck. They skidded to a halt. Johnno, with his hands on his knees, panted, ‘What was that?’

Laura didn’t have an answer. She approached the water’s edge carefully, blinking the last white dots of exertion from her eyes. The water was slack again now, almost glassy. She peered into the shadows; a hundred eyes glinted back at her. She caught herself, calling out to Johnno, ‘Fish,’ she called back. ‘Dead fish. Hundreds of them. Just, dead.’ She wondered for a moment what kind they were. They looked silver in the moonlight, no more than ten centimetres long and five wide. Her mind made up, she waded toward them, the icy water making her grimace as it flooded her boots.

‘Careful!’ Johnno cautioned. ‘Laura. Please. Don’t go any deeper.’

She reached into the water and pulled one of them out. She realised now why they were not floating. They had been crumpled, sucked dry. Dehydrated. Like nothing she had ever seen before. She tossed it toward Johnno. ‘Get a photo of that.’

He grimaced, his face tightening as he realised the strangeness of the situation. His hand shook slightly as he placed it in the shot as a reference for size. ‘Do you think, whatever did this, also…’ he trailed off, not wanting to finish the thought.

Laura was now searching around the area where the fish lay, the stirred sand turning the water a milky white. Her brow was furrowed as she concentrated hard, looking for anything, any sign of evidence or reason that could explain what they had just seen. A flash of red caught her eye. She leapt backwards—

‘What?’ Johnno looked up from his work.

‘Nothing—I just thought.’ She took a breath and waded back in, toeing the sand away where she had seen it. There was definitely something there. She could feel it now; it sagged and caught the water as she worked it free. It was a cap—bright red, with a gold lightning bolt. Johnno took another photograph, his Adam’s apple bobbing noiselessly in his throat.

Laura raised the tape recorder to her lips, her finger quivering over the red record button. After a moment, she lowered it again and looked directly at Johnno. ‘I think we need to call Dad.’
